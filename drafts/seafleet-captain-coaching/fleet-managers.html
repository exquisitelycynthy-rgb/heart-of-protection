The business case

Your exposure sits on a handful of bridges, a few minutes at a time.

Berth and quay contacts, tug incidents and near-misses in confined waters drive a share of claims, off-hire and vetting findings that's far bigger than the time involved.

Certificates show that officers are qualified. They don't show how a master handles this vessel on this berth in 25 knots of crosswind. That gap is what auditors, charterers and underwriters are increasingly asking about.

  • Claims and deductiblesBerth damage, fender and tug claims, and the management time each one costs.
  • Off-hire and schedule disruptionIncident investigations, repairs and port-state attention.
  • Vetting and charterer confidenceObservations on bridge team management and master–pilot exchange.
  • Retention and successionPromoting chief officers to first command without a structured bridge to get there.

Questions

What fleet managers ask.

How much time does it take from our officers?

Most coaching happens remotely around watch schedules. On-board sessions are planned around port calls you already have, so there's no extra off-hire.

What do we see about individual officers?

We agree on reporting before we start. Typically you receive competence records and fleet-level findings, and the coaching conversations themselves stay confidential so officers can be candid.

How does this fit with our existing training?

It complements simulator and statutory training. We align deliverables to your SMS and training matrix rather than adding a parallel system.

Can the output be used for vetting and audits?

The records are designed to serve as evidence of competence development. We'd confirm exactly how they map to your vetting and audit requirements during the briefing.

How do we measure whether it worked?

We set measures at the start, such as competence baseline vs. end-of-programme, incident and near-miss trends, and vetting observations, and report against them.
